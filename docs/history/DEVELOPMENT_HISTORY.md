# Societies Development History

## 1. Broad exploration before V3

Early development expanded across jobs, intentions, needs, organizations, homesteads, markets, factions, multiplayer ideas, world generation, crafting, and other society systems. Individual tasks often looked plausible, but the work lacked a single smallest product proof and reliable execution gates. Multiple Codex branches repeated or repaired similar tasks, plans described systems that did not exist, and many changes were reported without running the game or full tests.

This period was not worthless. It created design vocabulary, exposed technical requirements, and taught the project what uncontrolled horizontal expansion looks like. But it produced far less cumulative product progress than its activity suggested.

## 2. V3 stabilization

V3 established the first dependable substrate: Godot 4 + C#, deterministic session ownership, command/event boundaries, persistence, replay, diagnostics, unit tests, Godot headless tests, and stronger performance evidence. Broken refactors and stale scene references were repaired, status claims became more explicit, and the project began separating mechanical validation from product acceptance.

V3 was not the final vision. It was the first foundation from which later work could accumulate instead of repeatedly restarting.

## 3. Snow Globe laboratory

Snow Globe reframed the problem as a deliberately small deterministic world with persistent agents, immutable observations, provider-neutral inference, validated proposals, controlled scheduling, checkpoint/resume, recorded responses, replay, metrics, quality comparison, and failure recovery. This phase was much more unified than pre-V3 work and directly addressed the requirements of persistent model-assisted citizens.

The lab also developed very deep provider, storage, financial-journal, readiness, routing, and recovery machinery. Much of it is technically strong. Its danger is proportionality: infrastructure can continue to deepen after the next product risk has shifted to embodied citizens and social consequence.

## 4. Product rejection and recovery

The old player-facing prototype and an initial HUD/interaction recovery were rejected by the owner despite passing technical checks. They remained too basic, repetitive, on rails, and disconnected from the desired experience. This was an important correction: automated correctness stopped being treated as proof that Societies was becoming a good game.

The recovery planning locked the embodied resident-founder, autonomous citizen compact, wetland settlement, and negotiated water-control consequence. Voxel grounding and worldcraft work then created a more credible player-facing base.

## 5. EB-01R acceptance

On 2026-08-27 the owner accepted the bounded EB-01R founder-worldcraft slice for world readability, HUD hierarchy, inventory usability, construction clarity, and interaction feel. This is the first clearly accepted player-facing step in the current direction. It expands and refines the V3 foundation rather than discarding it.

The acceptance remains bounded. The scene has no participating citizens, and the project has not yet demonstrated the central social/ecological loop.

## 6. Repository consolidation

The accepted source state was preserved at commit `847c86b1c379e6a1dd8d4b7b641c3c89646e28c9` and branch `archive/pre-consolidation-2026-08-27`. Consolidation then began to replace giant append-only status files, many simultaneously active plans, and stacked delivery ambiguity with one repository, one authority chain, one active milestone, concise current documents, historical archives, scoped agent rules, and automated governance checks.

## Lessons made permanent

1. A large amount of technically valid work can still be product tire-spinning.
2. The smallest complete product proof must control the roadmap.
3. One active milestone is more valuable than many detailed plans.
4. Execution agents should not select their own next adjacent task.
5. Human acceptance is authoritative for experience claims.
6. Deterministic authority, persistence, replay, and fallback are foundational and worth preserving.
7. Provider and reliability work is valuable only in proportion to the product risk it resolves.
8. Historical work should be preserved, but never allowed to masquerade as current authority.
